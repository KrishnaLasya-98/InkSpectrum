 Temporary teeth
The temporary teeth are also called Milk Teeth. A new-born child has no 
teeth. Milk teeth begin to grow when a baby is about six months old and 
continue to grow for three years. We get 20 milk teeth in all – 10 in the 
upper jaw and 10 in the lower jaw.
Temporary Teeth
Permanent teeth
Milk teeth begins to fall one by one between the ages of six to twelve. The 
teeth that grow in place of milk teeth are called Permanent Teeth. There 
are 32 permanent teeth – 16 in the upper jaw and 16 in the lower jaw.
Permanent Teeth
Kinds of teeth
We have four kinds of permanent teeth – incisors, canines, premolars and 
molars. The shape of each tooth helps it to carry out different functions.

Incisors
The chisel-shaped four front teeth in 
both the upper and lower jaws are called 
Incisors. Incisors are flat but sharp at the 
ends. They help us to bite and cut food into 
smaller pieces. Incisors are the first teeth 
that a child gets. 
 
Canines
On either sides of incisors, there are 
sharp and pointed teeth. These are called 
Canines. There are four canines, two in 
each jaw. They help us to tear food into 
pieces. 
Premolars
There are eight Premolars, four in each 
jaw. They grow next to canines. They are 
wide and have ridges in them. They help 
us to cut and grind food. 
 
Molars
On either sides of premolars, there are 12 
Molars, six in each jaw. They are wider 
than premolars and have more ridges in 
them. They help us to crush and grind food 
before we swallow it.

Structure of tooth
Each tooth has two main parts, the crown and the root – separated by a thin 
narrow neck. The crown is the top part of the tooth. It is seen above the gum. 
The root is the lower part of the tooth. It cannot be seen as it is inside the 
gum. It holds the tooth firmly in place. The neck is surrounded by the gum. 
A tooth has three layers. 
•	
The outer white layer is called the Enamel. The enamel is the hardest 
part of the body. 
•	
The middle layer of the tooth that is slightly yellow is called the 
Dentine. This is softer than the enamel. 
•	
The innermost layer of the tooth is called the Pulp. The pulp is soft 
and has blood vessels and nerves. The nutrients reach the tooth through 
these blood vessels.

Taking Care of Teeth and Gums
Healthy teeth are a part of a healthy body. Our teeth, along with the tongue 
and saliva, help us to chew food so that it can be swallowed and digested 
easily. We should take care of our teeth. 
•	
Brush your teeth twice a day, once in the morning and once at night 	
	
before going to bed. 
•	
Gently massage the gums with finger.
•	
Use dental floss to clean between the teeth.
•	
Clean your tongue also to remove germs.
•	
Rinse your mouth after every meal.
•	
Do not use pins or sharp objects to clean your teeth.
Healthy Diet for Healthy Teeth
What we eat determines the health of our teeth and gums.
Food rich in calcium such as milk, yogurt, cheese, almonds and eggs keep 
the enamel of our teeth strong. Fibrous foods such as vegetables and fruits 
help to keep our teeth and gums strong.  
Food rich in vitamins C and D make our teeth strong. Foods such as oranges, 
lemons, tomatoes and broccoli are rich sources of vitamin C. Vitamin D is 
present in fish, wheat and eggs.
We should avoid sugary food. Sugar stays on the teeth for a long time. It 
allows germs to grow. Soft drinks, such as colas, damage the enamel of our 
teeth.

Healthy Habits for Healthy Teeth
Crunchy foods such as apples and carrots brush away food particles from 
the teeth, just like tooth brush. The saliva produced when we eat an apple 
or carrot washes away bacteria and food particles. 
The fibrous fruits or vegetables also stimulate our gums and keep them 
healthy.
A dentist is a doctor who takes care of gums and teeth. We should visit a 
dentist once in six months. The dentist checks our teeth for cavities. She/he 
also checks our gums. Regular visits to a dentist can help to prevent damage 
to our teeth and gums. 
Formation of Cavities
If we take good care of our teeth, we can save them from decaying. When we 
eat, bits of the food remains stuck to the teeth. These can cause growth of 
germs. If we do not brush our teeth properly, a sticky layer called plaque 
develops on the teeth. Plaque is made up of bits of food, saliva and germs 
that live in the mouth. These germs feed on the bits of food stuck in the teeth 
and produce acids. 
 
Over the time, the acids eat through the enamel and make a hole called 
‘cavity’. If the cavity is not filled in time, it can cause further decay. When the 
germs attack the dentine and reach the pulp of the tooth, they cause severe 
pain. Tooth decay also results in bad breath, bleeding gums and indigestion. 
It may even cause the tooth to fall out.

The Second stage of Digestion
The Digestion System
The nutrients stored in the food we eat cannot be used by our body as 
they are. They have to be broken down into a simpler form. The process of 
breaking down of nutrients into a simpler form so that our body can absorb 
them easily is called digestion. The group of organs that work together to 
digest food forms the digestive system. The organs of the digestive system 
are the mouth, food pipe, stomach, small intestine, pancreas, liver and large 
intestine. Together they form alimentary canal.
 
Mouth and food pipe
The process of digestion begins in the mouth. You have learnt how healthy 
and strong teeth are important for chewing food and breaking into smaller 
pieces. 
Have you noticed how your mouth waters when you smell or see food? 
The liquid produced in the mouth is called  as saliva. When we eat food, 
the tongue helps to mix the food with the saliva and soften it. This makes it 
easy for the teeth to chew the food and break it into smaller pieces. Saliva 
also begins to break down the starch (carbohydrates) present in the food 
into soluble sugar. The food then passes into the food pipe. The food pipe 
is also called the oesophagus. From the food pipe, the food enters into the 
stomach.

Stomach
The stomach is a hollow, muscular bag-like structure. The strong muscles 
of the stomach churn the food into even smaller pieces. The digestive juices 
including hydrochloric acid in the stomach help to break down the food into a 
simpler form. Food is stored in the stomach for 3 to 4 hours before it reaches 
the small intestine.
Small Intestine
From the stomach the food passes into a long coiled tube called the small 
intestine. Here, the food mixes with digestive juices from the small intestine, 
liver and pancreas. The food is now completely digested. 
The nutrients in the food are absorbed by blood vessels in the walls of the 
small intestine. The blood then carries the nutrients of the digested food to 
the different parts of the body. 
Large Intestine, Rectum and Anus
The undigested food in the small intestine passes into the large intestine 
(colon). The large intestine absorbs water from this undigested food. What 
is left behind is a semi-solid waste called faeces. This moves to the rectum 
and passes out of the body through the anus.

Need for Chewing Food Well
We should chew our food well before swallowing it. Chewing helps to break 
down large pieces of food into smaller ones, making it easier to digest. If we 
do not chew our food well, large pieces of food can take a longer time to get 
digested. 
Our mouth produces saliva when we chew food. Saliva contains digestive 
juices that begin the process of digestion when it mixes with the food. Saliva 
also cleans our teeth by washing away bits of food in the mouth.
The muscles of our mouth get exercised when we chew food. Also, the longer 
we chew, the more time it takes to finish a meal. This prevents over-eating. 
We can enjoy the taste of our food when we take the time to chew it.
Need for Water
Water is essential for the body. It helps the body to digest food properly. 
It helps to remove waste from the body. Water also helps to maintain the 
temperature of the body. We should drink at least 8 to 10 glasses of water.
When our body is properly hydrated, more water is absorbed by the large 
intestine. This keeps faeces soft and easy to pass.
